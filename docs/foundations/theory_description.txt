# Deterministic Word-to-Concept Mapping in a Hesperia-Style Hierarchy and the Role of a 72k Hex Layer

## Problem framing and why science text “works better”

You’re describing an architecture where text is converted into structured “maps” (activation or evidence patterns) across a hierarchy: **wording → concepts → families → areas**, with an intermediate **hex substrate** used to stabilize, spread, and shape the signal before it is aggregated upward.

A blunt reality: the hard part is not “families” or “areas.” Those are *designed* (you can specify them). The hard part is *deterministically* converting messy natural language (especially spoken, informal speech) into stable **concept evidence** that is (a) precise enough to be meaningful and (b) consistent enough to be used as raw_text to dynamics instead of as random noise.

Science/technical text often behaves better under dictionary/ontology-driven pipelines because it is more likely to use **domain-stable terminology** and because many domains already have **controlled vocabularies** and hierarchical term systems. In biomedicine, for example, literature is indexed with **MeSH** (a controlled, hierarchical thesaurus), and the same surface string can be systematically mapped (or disambiguated) against curated concept inventories. citeturn15search20turn15search19

## What “deterministic” must mean in practice

Determinism here can’t just mean “no ML.” It must mean:

1. **One raw_text → one output** under fixed versioned resources (lexicons, ontologies, rules, and tie-breakers).
2. **Explainable trace**: for every concept hit, you can show *which span matched which rule* and *why one sense won over alternatives*.
3. **Regression-testable behavior**: new dictionary entries shouldn’t silently change prior classifications unless intended (this is how LIWC-style systems remain operationally useful across versions). citeturn14view0turn13search6

A strong anchor example is **LIWC**: it processes text word-by-word, matches to dictionary entries (including word stems), and increments one or more categories per match. That is deterministic by design, and it demonstrates the core idea of “preset families/categories + wired dictionary.” citeturn14view0turn17view1turn13search6

The catch: LIWC categories are “close enough” because it accepts that a word can map to multiple categories (e.g., “cried” increments several), and it is *statistics-first* (category rates, not perfect semantic truth). citeturn14view0turn17view1  
If you want concept-level truth (or clean family/area attribution) for free speech, you must add **disambiguation and phrase handling**.

## Closing the wording → concept gap deterministically

### Controlled concept inventory plus SKOS/ontology discipline

The cleanest deterministic bridge is: treat “concepts” as **explicit identifiers** in a controlled scheme, and treat “wording” as one of many **lexical labels** that can point to those concepts.

This is exactly the problem space addressed by the knowledge-organization community: **concept schemes**, hierarchical relations (broader/narrower), and explicit mapping relations like “exact match” vs “close match.” The **entity["organization","World Wide Web Consortium","standards body"] SKOS** model is a practical reference point because it formalizes:
- a concept as an idea/meaning unit,
- preferred/alternative/hidden labels (synonyms, spelling variants),
- and relations between concepts plus cross-scheme mapping properties. citeturn6search3turn6search1

That structure is exactly what you need if your “families” and “areas” are higher-level groupings: you want **lexical labels → concept IDs → broader concept IDs → family/area membership**.

### Deterministic candidate generation: don’t start with single words

Single-word mapping is where ambiguity lives. Your deterministic execution_flow should be **phrase-first, then word fallback**:

- Identify candidate spans (n-grams, noun phrases, key patterns).
- Prefer the **longest match** in your lexicon (“structural interview” should beat “structural” + “interview”).
- Only if no span matches, fall back to single tokens/stems.

This is not optional: multiword expressions are known to be a core pain point in NLP precisely because meaning regularly crosses word boundaries (“pain in the neck” is literally the canonical example). citeturn15search10turn15search16

### Deterministic normalization layer: make surface forms boring

Before any concept lookup, apply a strictly deterministic normalization execution_flow:

- Unicode normalization, case-folding, punctuation policy (define it; don’t wing it).
- Tokenization plus a **conservative** contraction policy for speech (“I’m” → “I” + “am”).
- Lemmatization or stemming (rule-based or frozen-model; but freeze the toolchain versions).
- Domain normalization tables (“PTSD” ↔ “post-traumatic stress disorder”, etc.).

LIWC explicitly supports word stems via * (e.g., hungr*) to capture morphological families deterministically; that idea generalizes cleanly to your concept lexicon as well. citeturn17view1turn14view0

### Deterministic disambiguation: you need a tie-break system that is *part of the architecture*

Once lexical lookup returns multiple possible concepts, you must disambiguate. If you don’t, your higher layers (hex → family → area) will be driven by arbitrary noise and your “maps” will not be stable.

A practical deterministic approach is **knowledge-based word sense disambiguation (WSD)**:

- WSD is explicitly defined as selecting the intended meaning of a word in context. citeturn7search21turn7search6
- Classic dictionary-based WSD begins with comparing overlaps between context words and sense definitions (the Lesk family of methods). citeturn22search36turn8search12
- A known improvement is to use a semantic network like **WordNet** so that overlap can be expanded along semantic relations rather than being limited to short gloss text. The adapted Lesk line of work is based on exactly this idea. citeturn22search14turn7search0

**WordNet** itself is built around synsets: sets of cognitive synonyms, each representing a distinct concept, connected via semantic relations. citeturn7search0turn7search5  
That is a near-direct analogue of what you want: “surface strings → concept(s) → relations → higher grouping.”

A deterministic scoring + tie-break recipe that works in practice:

- **Match strength**: exact phrase match > lemma match > stem match > fuzzy match.
- **Context overlap** (Lesk-like): overlap between local context tokens and (a) concept definition text, (b) concept synonyms, (c) related concept labels. citeturn22search36turn22search14
- **Hierarchy coherence**: prefer candidates whose families/areas are already “activated” by other nearby unambiguous hits (this is deterministic if you define the window and weights).
- **Specificity bias**: if two candidates tie, choose the more specific concept (deeper in the hierarchy).
- **Stable priors**: if still tied, choose a fixed default ordering (frequency priors are allowed if fixed and versioned).

This is not “probabilistic learning.” It’s deterministic ranking under fixed weights.

### Deterministic “flexible matching” for domain terminology (why scientific text is easier)

In technical domains, robust matching is often built by combining:
- shallow parsing / noun phrase extraction,
- then flexible string matching against a controlled concept inventory.

A concrete example: compute MEDLINE at scale by extracting noun phrases and matching them to **UMLS Metathesaurus** names using flexible matching techniques. citeturn15search19  
Even when coverage isn’t complete, the method is systematic: you can see which strings matched which concepts, and which did not (exactly what a deterministic audit trail needs). citeturn15search19turn15search20

For your use case (random speech), you essentially have to build an equivalent “controlled vocabulary + matching + disambiguation” layer—but tuned to **colloquial paraphrase**, **disfluency**, and **metaphor**.

## Building “maps” of relation once you have concept evidence

Once text is converted into concept-level evidence, you can build measurable “maps” in several deterministic ways—some lexical/statistical, some graph/dynamic.

### Text-to-concept salience: TF‑IDF-style weighting

If you want “relation to a certain text,” you usually want **salience**: which concepts are characteristic of this text relative to others. The classic deterministic baseline is TF‑IDF. citeturn15search3turn15search0  
This gives you stable per-concept weights that can feed downstream projections (concept → hex → family → area) without requiring any learning.

### Concept-to-concept association: PMI and co-occurrence structure

If you want “maps of words/concepts and their relations,” you typically want association measures. Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) is a standard, deterministic association score used in lexicography and collocation discovery. citeturn15search4turn15search9  
This becomes especially useful for:
- finding stable concept pairs that co-occur in a domain,
- building deterministic edges in your concept graph,
- and supporting disambiguation (“which meaning is consistent with nearby concepts?”).

### Hierarchical projections to families and areas

Once concept hits are stable, projecting upward is straightforward if:
- each concept has one or more family memberships,
- families roll up into areas via explicit mappings.

This is essentially how hierarchical category systems work in tools like LIWC (lower-level categories nesting into broader ones), but you’d be doing it at a concept-graph level rather than a wordlist level. citeturn17view1turn14view0

The key reliability point: **don’t let “family” be the first semantic decision point.**  
Family should be a projection of concept evidence, not a replacement for concept evidence. Otherwise, any ambiguity at the word level leaks upward and becomes “family drift.”

## The 72k hex layer: what it is good for in a Hesperia-style system

A hex substrate is not decoration. It can be the difference between:
- a brittle “bag of words → category counts” execution_flow, and
- a stable multiscale semantic field that supports smoothing, locality, and trajectories.

Because your question explicitly calls out “72k cells,” the practical question is: what do you *buy* with that resolution?

### Why hex grids are used at all

Hex grids are popular for measurement because neighbor relationships are uniform: each hex has a single “type” of adjacency and equal neighbor distance, simplifying smoothing and gradient-style operations compared to square grids (which have edge-neighbors and corner-neighbors at different distances). citeturn8search2turn8search10  

This is a core design motivation behind **H3**, a hexagonal hierarchical indexing system built at entity["company","Uber","rideshare company"]: it explicitly highlights hexagon neighbor uniformity and the benefits for measurement/smoothing over gradients. citeturn8search2turn8search13

### What a 72k-cell layer can do that families/areas can’t

At a high level, a hex layer can serve four distinct purposes, and you can use all four simultaneously.

**Local smoothing / denoising (stability for noisy speech)**  
Free speech is sparse and messy. A hex layer lets you spread activation locally (“semantic heat”) so that small lexical perturbations don’t massively change which region is activated. This is the same logic as diffusion on networks: with adjacency matrix \(A\), degree matrix \(D\), Laplacian \(L = D - A\), diffusion dynamics can be written in forms like \(\frac{dc}{dt} = -\alpha Lc\). citeturn21search4turn21search37  
That matters because it turns “spiky hits” into “regions,” and regions are what stable higher-level classification likes.

**Microstate capacity (sub-structure inside a family)**  
Families (and areas) are coarse. A 72k substrate offers room for:
- sub-concepts,
- stylistic variants,
- intensity variations,
- and “neighboring-but-not-identical” meanings
to live near each other without collapsing into one family bin.

In practice, you use this to avoid the classic failure mode of lexicon classifiers: everything becomes “some category,” but you can’t tell whether it’s the same type of evidence or just the same bucket.

**Trajectory features (classification beyond word counts)**  
If each text (or speech segment) induces a sequence of activations over the hex layer, you can compute deterministic “movement” features:
- how concentrated vs diffuse the activation is,
- how fast it shifts between regions,
- whether it stabilizes into a basin (interpretation/integration-like behavior) or remains scattered (chaotic-like behavior).

This is where your hex layer becomes useful for staged classification goals (your TFP example). It’s not that the hex layer “knows TFP.” It’s that it supports *time-structured features* that are hard to represent in families/areas alone.

For context, **TFP (Transference-Focused Psychotherapy)** is typically described as a structured psychodynamic treatment approach; clinical trials and reviews describe it as manualized and structured, and it has been studied in randomized controlled designs. citeturn16search0turn16search4turn16search2  
So if your “TFP test” is about classifying speech into therapy-relevant categories or stages, trajectory-style features are a rational target (more principled than raw word counts).

**Deterministic indexing/bucketing (geometry as a stable hash)**  
A hex grid can also be used as a deterministic bucketing scheme: concepts map into hex cells (or small neighborhoods) based on fixed rules (e.g., concept ID hashing + topology constraints + family anchors). The point is not cryptography; the point is repeatable spatial organization that supports neighborhood operations.

H3 demonstrates this general idea in geospatial form: points/areas map into stable hex cells which can then be aggregated, compacted, and used for neighborhood queries. citeturn8search13turn8search7turn8search10  
In Hesperia, the analogous value is: stable indexing makes it easy to define deterministic “locality,” “rings,” and “distance” between semantic cells.

## Practical blueprint: how to implement the gap-filling without fooling yourself

A workable deterministic architecture usually needs three explicit artifacts (versioned, testable) plus one discipline:

### Lexical-concept wiring artifact

A “dictionary” is not just a wordlist. It should be a structured lexicon with:

- Multiword entries (preferred).
- Synonym sets (alt labels).
- “Hidden labels” for misspellings/transcription variants (SKOS explicitly supports hidden labels as a concept). citeturn6search1turn6search3
- Explicit negative patterns (to block false friends).
- Explicit concept IDs (not strings).

This is the lesson from LIWC: counting works when dictionary entries are curated, assigned to categories, and include stems; you adopt the same discipline but attach entries to concept IDs. citeturn17view1turn14view0

### Deterministic resolver artifact

Implement a resolver that outputs, for each matched span:

- span boundaries,
- candidate concepts,
- chosen concept,
- score breakdown,
- and applied tie-break rules.

Then test it like you would test a compiler.

### Calibration corpus and regression tests

You need a curated set of short texts where you *know* the intended concept mappings (even if only for a subset of spans). Without this, you can’t tell if your hex/family dynamics are “working” or just amplifying garbage.

This is also why scientific text looks “better”: established corpora + controlled vocabularies make it easier to define what “correct mapping” even means. citeturn15search20turn15search19

### Ontology discipline for families/areas

Use an explicit knowledge-organization pattern (SKOS-like is fine) so you can answer:

- Which concept belongs to which family?
- Are family relations broader/narrower or associative?
- Are cross-family relations allowed and how are they weighted?

This prevents the “everything is related to everything” problem, which kills deterministic classification because it makes the mapping underconstrained. citeturn6search1turn6search3

## Bottom-line answers to your two questions

Your core problem is not “how do I align words to families?” It’s “how do I map *phrases in context* to *stable concept IDs* deterministically.” The most reliable deterministic approach is:

- build a controlled concept inventory with disciplined lexical labels (SKOS-style),
- do phrase-first matching with deterministic normalization,
- apply knowledge-based disambiguation (WordNet/WSD-style) with explicit scoring and tie-breaks,
- and only then project to families/areas and let the hex substrate smooth/shape the signal. citeturn6search3turn7search0turn7search21turn22search14turn14view0turn17view1

For the 72k hex layer: its practical purpose is to provide **microstate capacity and locality** so you can do deterministic smoothing, region formation, and trajectory-based features—things that are either impossible or extremely brittle if you jump straight from concept hits to families/areas. Hex grids are specifically attractive because neighbor relationships are uniform and support clean smoothing/diffusion-style operations; this is a well-established motivation in hex-grid indexing systems like H3. citeturn8search2turn8search10turn8search13turn21search4turn21search37
